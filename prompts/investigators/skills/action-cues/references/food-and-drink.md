# Food, containers and utensils

| Action | Useful distinction to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| drink | Container reaches the mouth, often with a tilt or sustained rim contact, then lowers. Separate this from hand-to-mouth eating or medicine and from reaching for a nearby vessel. |
| eat | Repeated food/utensil movement toward the mouth from a plate, bowl or hand. Food identity can remain hidden. A utensil moving only within a bowl supports stirring more directly. |
| grab utensils | A hand approaches a resting implement, establishes a grip, then lifts/repositions it. Inspect frames before use; holding a spoon while stirring establishes use, not pickup timing. Retrieval may be very brief or initially hidden. |
| pour | A source vessel tips neck/rim downward toward another vessel and returns. Track object angle and target, not just an arm reaching across the table. Visible liquid is useful but not necessary for an acted pour. |
| stir | Repeated circular or back-and-forth implement motion within a container. A short bout can fit between pickup and eating; inspect consecutive frames. Reaching into a bowl once or merely holding a utensil is insufficient alone. |
| peel fruit | One hand stabilizes/rotates a small object while the other strips or picks at its surface; separating peel-like material can help. Banana-like peeling may begin by pinching/bending the stem or tip, then pulling successive strips downward to uncover the held fruit. Inspect the two hands' coordinated motion, including short acted peeling gestures, rather than requiring visible peel or exact fruit identity. Distinguish this from utensil pickup, rubbing or hand-to-mouth eating; do not infer peeling from holding food alone. |
| peel fruit with a knife | A separate tool makes controlled paring/scraping strokes against a held fruit-like object, often as the supporting hand rotates it. Compare with finger peeling and cutting/eating; an unresolved small tool does not justify asserting a visible blade. |

Follow the whole pickup → handling → use → release sequence, without inventing
missing steps. A grasp can precede the visually dominant action by several seconds.
Distinct actions may overlap or repeat. One ambiguous manipulation should not be
counted both as stirring and peeling unless separate physical evidence supports both.
A sequence question can suggest a hidden action is present, but cannot locate it.

For small two-handed food handling, actively test banana opening rather than
defaulting to generic tableware handling. One hand holding an object upright while
the other pinches/bends its tip and pulls downward along it is a sufficient acted
peeling pattern even if individual peel strips, fruit texture and exact shape are
unresolved. A short pull followed by a reset can suffice; a long repeated sequence
is not required. Inspect preparation between mouth contacts and before the first
bite: peeling and eating can alternate. Explain what physical motion favors utensil
handling if choosing that interpretation instead. Holding food or mouthward travel
alone is insufficient for peeling; if the relative motion is hidden, retain that
uncertainty rather than claiming it contradicts peeling.
